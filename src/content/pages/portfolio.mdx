## Why the record is the asset

Every business produces records nobody outside it can see — jobs, clients,
pricing, outcomes, what people asked for and what happened next. That exhaust is
generated whether or not anyone is paying attention to it.

In an ordinary build it lands in a vendor's database, in that vendor's schema,
and it leaves when the vendor does. Built the other way it accumulates on your
side, in a shape you can query, and it gets more valuable every year it runs.
That difference is the whole argument, and it costs about the same to build.

## What makes a record a moat rather than a pile of data

Five conditions. The more of them hold, the more the record is worth owning.

1. **A recurring stream nobody else sees.** Not a dataset you can buy. Records
   that exist because you did the work.
2. **Rules that are public but scattered.** Published by someone — a regulator,
   a council, an association — but spread across jurisdictions and formats
   nobody has pulled together.
3. **A price set administratively rather than by open bidding.** A formula, a
   schedule, a rate card. Where buyers and sellers publish what they paid, there
   is nothing left to know.
4. **Work that is tedious rather than technically hard.** Many small
   interpretation problems rather than one large engineering problem. That is
   the shape a national vendor never gets around to.
5. **Somebody keeping the history.** Not the current state — the sequence. Every
   other component can be acquired late. This one cannot be bought back at any
   price once the window has passed.

That is what the [diagnostic](/contact) scores, and why we can usually tell you
inside five questions whether this is worth doing for you.

## How one gets built

Three layers, in reverse order of glamour, because each is meaningless without
the one beneath it.

**The rulebook.** Whatever governs the market, normalised to machine-readable
form. Thirty jurisdictions, thirty formats, one output. The thirty-first is
nearly free; a new entrant redoes all thirty.

**The index.** The records attaching a rule to a specific thing — a parcel, a
job, a client, an account — keyed to whatever identifier the domain actually
uses, and durable.

**The events.** Listings, sales, jobs, transfers, outcomes. The layer everyone
wants to start with, and the one that means nothing without the two under it. An
event you cannot interpret is a row.

## How it gets paid for

**Publish existence, sell depth.** That a thing exists is usually the public
good, and giving it away costs almost nothing. The terms, the history, and the
view across everything at once are the product.

**Publish the rulebook in full.** It derives from public documents, so
withholding it captures almost no value, and publishing it makes you the
reference point for reading them. It also buys free proofreading.

**Never sell the history.** Sell findings derived from it, access to query it,
answers out of it. The moment the series itself changes hands, the only durable
thing about the position is gone.

**Access, not extraction.** A customer who can ask questions of the record
renews. A customer handed a copy of it does not.
